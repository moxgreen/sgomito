Spinta · notes from the workshop

Robots that playtest a game nobody has played yet

How a handful of simple programs, and one clever search algorithm, told us whether a new board game was broken before a single human sat down to play it.

Spinta (Italian for “push”) is a two-player game invented on paper a few weeks ago. Before asking friends to spend evenings on it, we wanted to know whether the rules were sound. Does the game end, or do players shuffle forever? Does the first player win too often? Is there a cheap trick that beats everything? And, the hardest question, does thinking harder actually help?

Human playtesters are slow and precious. Computers are fast and tireless, but they are only as good as the way they play. This article describes the bots we built, from a coin-flipper to a Monte Carlo tree search that discovers strategy on its own, and the arithmetic we used to decide whether to trust them.

The game in one minute

The board is a regular hexagon with five cells on each side: 61 cells, the same as Abalone. Each player starts with five pieces on their own edge, the home row. Indigo (player A) starts at the top and heads down; amber (player B) starts at the bottom and heads up. The four slanted edges are walls.

  • A turn is two moves, made with two different pieces. To compensate for moving first, A’s very first turn is a single move.
  • A move takes one piece to one of its six neighbouring cells. If that cell is occupied, by either colour, the piece there is pushed one cell further in the same direction, which must be empty and on the board. You never push two pieces, never push anything through a wall, and nothing is ever captured.
  • You win as soon as one of your pieces stands on the opponent’s home row.
  • Two anti-shuffling rules. You may not end your turn by restoring the position exactly as it was before your opponent’s last turn (a ko rule, borrowed from Go). And a piece that has just been pushed may not, on the very next turn, push its pusher straight back (no pushback).
  • A position repeated three times, or 200 single moves without a winner, is a draw.

Each of the rules in the last bullet is there because a bot found a hole in an earlier version. More on that below.

A ladder of bots

No single bot can answer every question. A bot that plays well is slow; a bot that is fast plays badly; a bot that plays one particular style tells you about that style and nothing else. So we built a ladder, and we did not climb to the next rung until the current one had answered its question.

Rung one: Random and Greedy

Random picks a legal move uniformly at random. It is useless as an opponent but invaluable as a test: if the rules engine has a bug, random play finds it quickly, because it visits strange positions no sensible player would reach. It also gives a baseline for every statistic. Two random players draw 45% of their games by hitting the 200-move limit; they push in 11% of their moves.

Greedy looks one move ahead and scores the result with a simple formula. Call the advancement of a piece the number of rows it has travelled from its own home row, from 0 to 8. Then

score = Σ advancement of my pieces − Σ advancement of the opponent’s pieces

and Greedy plays the move that raises this number most, breaking ties at random. A winning move scores +∞. Stepping a piece one row forward is worth +1; pushing an opponent’s piece one row back toward its home is also worth +1, and doing both at once is worth +2.

Greedy plays like a confident beginner. It tells us how long games last when both sides push forward, and how often the push is used. It cannot tell us whether the game is deep, because the formula is our own prejudice about what matters. If the formula is wrong, Greedy is wrong in exactly the same way every time.

Rung two: the saboteurs

The second rung holds bots that are deliberately bad at everything except one degenerate plan. Each one asks: would this cheap trick break the game?

  • Turtle never lets a piece leave its first two rows. It plugs holes and pushes away anything that comes near. If Turtle rarely loses, defence is too strong and the game will be a stalemate.
  • Rusher picks one piece and runs it forward as fast as possible, leaving the others at home. If Rusher wins, the game is a race and pushing does not matter.
  • Ping-pong plays like Greedy, except that whenever one of its pieces is pushed, it pushes straight back. It measures how much the game needs rules against endless back-and-forth.

Our rule of thumb: a sound game should let Greedy beat each saboteur at least 70% of the time. The saboteurs earned their keep:

  • In the first version of the rules, with no ko, two Greedy bots drew 67% of their games by threefold repetition. Pieces pushed each other back and forth until the position repeated. The ko rule fixed that.
  • With one move per turn, Rusher beat our strong search bot most of the time. The home row is an invulnerable wall (nothing can be pushed out through the edge behind it), so the defender’s pieces are safest when they do not move, and each defensive push draws a defender out of the wall. A runner in the second-to-last row threatens two goal cells at once. Allowing two moves per turn turned this around: now a defender can block and counter in the same turn, and the search bot beats Rusher about 95% of the time.
  • With two moves per turn the ko rule stopped catching ping-pong, because the other move of the turn changes the position, so it is never exactly restored. Between two Greedy bots, a third of all pushes simply undid the opponent’s last push. The no-pushback rule closes that gap.

Rung three: Monte Carlo tree search

The saboteurs check that the rules are not broken. To ask whether the game rewards thought, we need a bot that does not carry our prejudices: one that is given the rules and nothing else, and that plays better the longer it thinks. Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) is exactly that. It is the family of algorithms behind the first strong Go programs, before neural networks took over.

Judging a position by playing it out at random

Here is a surprising idea. To estimate how good a position is for you, play a few thousand games from it with both sides moving at random, and count how often you win. These random continuations are called playouts.

Random play is terrible, so why should this work at all? Because the errors are symmetric. If your position is genuinely better (more pieces near the goal, an open lane, a blocked opponent), it stays better on average across many random continuations, even though each one is silly. The win fraction p̂ after n playouts estimates a real number p, and the usual binomial arithmetic tells you how precisely:

standard error ≈ √( p(1 − p) / n )

Near p = 0.5, a thousand playouts give about ±1.6 percentage points. That is the budget we have to spread across all the moves we are considering.

Spending playouts wisely: the bandit problem

Suppose there are 20 legal moves. Splitting the playouts evenly wastes most of them on moves that are obviously bad after a few tries. Pouring them all into the current favourite risks never noticing that a move which looked poor early on was actually the best.

This is the multi-armed bandit problem: a row of slot machines with unknown payout rates, and a limited number of coins. A classical solution, called UCB1, plays the machine that maximises

wi / ni + c · √( ln N / ni )

where ni is how often move i has been tried, wi how many of those tries were wins, and N the total number of tries. The first term rewards moves that have done well (exploitation). The second term is large for moves that have been tried rarely (exploration). Because ln N keeps growing, no move is abandoned forever; because it grows slowly, the bad moves get only a logarithmic share. The constant c sets the balance between the two.

From one move to a whole tree

UCB1 chooses among the moves available now. MCTS applies the same rule recursively, growing a tree of positions in memory. Each simulation goes through four steps:

  1. Selection. Start at the current position and repeatedly step to the child with the highest UCB score, until you reach a position with a move that has never been tried.
  2. Expansion. Try that move, and add the resulting position to the tree.
  3. Simulation. From there, play randomly to the end of the game.
  4. Backpropagation. Walk back up the path, adding one visit to every node and adding the result (1 for a win, 0 for a loss, ½ for a draw) from the point of view of the player who chose that node.

Repeat this thousands of times. The tree grows unevenly: deep along lines that look promising for both players, shallow elsewhere. Near the root, the statistics become a minimax-like judgement (“this move looks good, unless the reply that the tree has learned to prefer refutes it”), while far below the tree the random playouts supply a rough estimate. When the time is up, the bot plays the move whose node was visited most often. Visit counts are more robust than average results, because a move with a high average from only a few visits may just have been lucky.

Notice what is absent: there is no evaluation formula. Nobody told the bot that advancing is good or that a wall at home is useful. Whatever it believes, it found out from the outcomes of games.

Two adjustments that made a large difference

The textbook version, with c ≈ 1.4 and purely random playouts, played poorly here. It beat Greedy only about two times in three, and its visit counts were spread almost evenly over all moves. Two changes fixed it.

Decisive moves. In a purely random playout, a player with a piece one step from the goal wins only if the coin happens to choose that step. A position where you are about to win and one where you are about to lose then look nearly alike, and every estimate drifts toward 50%. So in our playouts, a player who can win immediately always does. This is not strategy, just the rules: “if you can win now, win”. It makes playouts far more informative, at the cost of checking for winning moves at every step, which we keep cheap with a precomputed table of which cells and directions can reach the goal.

A smaller exploration constant. Even with decisive moves, playout results in Spinta are noisy and the differences between candidate moves are small, typically a few percentage points. With c = 1.4 the exploration term swamps those differences and the search never commits. We use c = 0.5.

Book-keeping: repetitions and ko

Ko and repetition rules depend on history, not only on the current position, so the search must remember which positions have occurred. Storing and comparing whole positions would be slow. Instead each position gets a 62-bit fingerprint by Zobrist hashing: assign a random 62-bit number to every (colour, cell) pair, and XOR together the numbers of all occupied cells. Because XOR is its own inverse, moving a piece updates the fingerprint with just two XORs: remove the old cell, add the new one. Two different positions collide only by bad luck; with a few million positions in play, the chance of any collision is around 2−20.

Two moves per turn add a small twist. The tree no longer alternates strictly between players, since one player often makes two consecutive moves. Each node simply remembers who moved into it, and the result is credited to that player.

Does thinking pay? The doubling test

A deep game is one where a better player keeps beating a slightly worse one, at every level of skill. Chess has this property; tic-tac-toe does not, since after a few minutes of thought everyone plays perfectly and every game is a draw. MCTS lets us test this directly: the number of simulations per move is a dial for how hard the bot thinks.

We play a bot against a copy of itself with twice the simulations: 100 against 200, 200 against 400, and so on, 200 games per pairing, with colours alternating. The results are expressed as Elo differences. If the stronger side scores a fraction p of the points, the Elo difference is

D = 400 · log10( p / (1 − p) )

so 64% is about +100, 76% about +200, and 50% is 0. The scale is additive: if A is 100 Elo above B and B is 100 above C, A should score about 76% against C.

The signature of a shallow game is a gain that shrinks with every doubling, heading to zero once both sides play “well enough”. A deep game shows a roughly constant gain per doubling. Here is what Spinta gave, with the current rules:

Making it fast enough

All of this is written in plain Python, with no libraries. Python is slow, so a few tricks matter.

  • PyPy. The same code run with PyPy, a Python implementation with a just-in-time compiler, plays out random games about ten times faster.
  • A flat board for playouts. The rules engine uses readable, immutable positions. The search copies the position into a plain array of 61 cells with precomputed neighbour tables, and updates it in place.
  • Uniform random moves without listing them. To pick a random legal move, pick a random (piece, direction) pair out of the 5 × 6 = 30 and try again if it is illegal. Conditioned on being accepted, every legal move is equally likely, so the result is exactly uniform, and on average it takes 30 / (number of legal moves) tries. That is much cheaper than generating the full list of legal moves at every step.
  • Several trees at once. The strongest level on the web version runs independent searches on every processor core of your device and adds up their visit counts for each move at the root. The trees never talk to each other, which is wasteful, but it is simple and it scales.

What the robots cannot tell us

The bots can rule out the ways a game can be broken: endless draws, a decisive first-move advantage, a single cheap plan that beats everything, or a ceiling beyond which thinking stops paying. Spinta, with its current rules, passes all of these tests. A companion article looks at what the strongest bots actually do when they play each other: how they open, and whether their games all look alike.

What the bots cannot say is whether the game is fun: whether the pushes feel clever, whether a comeback feels possible, whether you want a rematch. For that we need people. The search bot, meanwhile, gets a second job: it is the opponent in the solo mode.
